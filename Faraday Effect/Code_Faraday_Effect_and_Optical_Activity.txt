\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================

The aim of this experiment is to investigate two optical
phenomena: the Faraday effect and natural optical activity.
Both phenomena involve the rotation of the plane of
polarization of linearly polarized light, although their
physical origins are different.

The Faraday effect, discovered by Michael Faraday in 1845,
is a magneto-optical phenomenon in which the plane of
polarization of light rotates as it propagates through a
transparent material subjected to a magnetic field parallel
to the direction of propagation. The rotation angle
$\phi$ is proportional to both the magnetic field strength
$B$ and the optical path length $L$ inside the material:

\begin{equation}
    \phi = VBL,
\end{equation}

where $V$ is the Verdet constant, a material-dependent
quantity that also depends on the wavelength of the
incident light.

The first part of the experiment consists of characterizing
the magnetic field generated by an electromagnet as a
function of the electric current flowing through it.
The Faraday rotation is then measured for different
magnetic field strengths using a blue optical filter.

In the second part, the wavelength dependence of the
Verdet constant is investigated using three optical
filters and three LEDs with different emission wavelengths.
This allows the experimental relationship between the
Verdet constant and the wavelength to be examined.

For flint glass, the Verdet constant can be approximated
by the following expression:

\begin{equation}
V(\lambda)=\frac{e\lambda}{2mc}
\frac{dn}{d\lambda},
\end{equation}

where $e$ is the elementary charge, $m$ is the electron
mass, $c$ is the speed of light in vacuum, and
$n(\lambda)$ is the refractive index of the material.

A typical dispersion model for flint glass is

\begin{equation}
n(\lambda)=n_0+\frac{n_1}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}

Differentiating with respect to wavelength gives

\begin{equation}
\frac{dn}{d\lambda}=-\frac{2n_1}{\lambda^3}.
\end{equation}

Substituting this expression into the Verdet constant
equation leads to

\begin{equation}
V(\lambda)=
\frac{e}{2mc}\frac{-2n_1}{\lambda^2}
\equiv \frac{C}{\lambda^2},
\end{equation}

where $C$ is a constant for a given material and
experimental configuration. Therefore, the Verdet
constant is expected to decrease approximately with
the inverse square of the wavelength.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.465\textwidth]{intro.png}
    \caption{Schematic representation of the Faraday effect.
    The rotation angle $\phi$ is proportional to the magnetic
    field $B$ and the optical path length $L$ inside the
    material. The proportionality constant is the Verdet
    constant $V(\lambda)$.}
    \label{fig:faraday_principle}
\end{figure}

Finally, the natural optical activity of two sugar solutions
is studied using a Laurent polarimeter. Unlike the Faraday
effect, this rotation occurs naturally in optically active
materials and does not require an external magnetic field.

\subsection{Rotation Angle as a Function of the Magnetic Field}

Using the blue filter, the rotation angle was measured
for different current values, reversing the current
direction at each measurement point.

Since the current intensities recorded for the two
polarities were not exactly equal, the average current
was used:

\[
I=\frac{I_+ + I_-}{2}.
\]

The average rotation angle was calculated as

\begin{equation}
    \phi=\frac{|\phi_+|+|\phi_-|}{2},
    \qquad
    B=(18.9061\ \mathrm{mT/A})I.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Verdet Constant at Different Wavelengths}

The Verdet constant was determined for the three optical
filters and the three LEDs by using an approximately
constant current of $I_F=9.0$ A.

As in the previous section, the average rotation angles
were calculated by reversing the current direction.
According to the magnetic field calibration, the
corresponding magnetic field was approximately

\[
B_F\simeq170.15\ \mathrm{mT}.
\]

For each wavelength, the Verdet constant was calculated

\begin{equation}
    V(\lambda)=\frac{60\,\phi}{B_FL},
\end{equation}

where the factor 60 converts degrees into arcminutes.

\subsection{Determination of the Constant C}

To investigate the spectral dependence of the Verdet
constant, the following relationship is considered:

\begin{equation}
    \phi(\lambda)=V(\lambda)B_FL,
    \qquad
    V(\lambda)=\frac{C}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}

Therefore, plotting the rotation angle $\phi$ as a
function of $x=1/\lambda^2$ allows a linear fit through
the origin to be performed.

The slope $m$ and the constant $C$ are given by

\begin{equation}
    m=\frac{\sum_i x_i\phi_i}{\sum_i x_i^2},
    \qquad
    C=\frac{60m}{B_FL}.
\end{equation}

Using the six experimental measurements, the slope is

\[
m\simeq6.5614\times10^6\
^\circ\mathrm{nm}^2.
\]

Taking $B_F=170.15$ mT and $L=2.0$ cm, the resulting
value of the constant is

\[
\boxed{
C\simeq1.16\times10^6\
\frac{\mathrm{min}\,\mathrm{nm}^2}
{\mathrm{mT}\,\mathrm{cm}}
}.
\]

\subsection{Comparison with Literature Values}

Using the previous fit, the Verdet constant can be
estimated at the two requested wavelengths according
to $V(\lambda)=C/\lambda^2$.

As a reference, published values for TG20 glass
were found in the literature \cite{crylink}. However,
it has not been possible to verify whether these values
are directly applicable to the TGP20 glass specified
in the laboratory instructions. Consequently, the
following comparison should be regarded as indicative
rather than as a quantitative validation of the
material under investigation.

The reference reports $V_{632.8}=-0.258$ and
$V_{1064}=-0.075$ min/(Oe cm). These values were
converted using $1\ \mathrm{Oe}\simeq0.1\ \mathrm{mT}$.
The absolute values were compared because the
experimental measurements were recorded without
considering the sign of the rotation.

The resulting relative deviations are approximately
12\% and 36\%, respectively.

\subsection{Measurement of Natural Optical Activity}

Finally, a Laurent polarimeter was used to investigate
the optical activity of two sugar solutions.

The measurement consists of adjusting the brightness
of the two semicircular fields until they appear equal,
first without a sample and then with each sugar solution
placed in the optical path.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Sample & Rotation $\Delta\theta$ ($^\circ$) &
Length $l$ (cm)\\
\midrule
A & 7.4 & 20.8\\
B & 7.9 & 20.8\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Measured rotations relative to the reference
without a sample and corresponding solution lengths.}
\label{tab:sucre}
\end{table}

The optical rotation per unit length is calculated as

\begin{equation}
    P=\frac{\Delta\theta}{l}.
\end{equation}

Therefore, the following values are obtained:

\[
P_A=\frac{7.4^\circ}{20.8\ \mathrm{cm}}
=0.36\ ^\circ/\mathrm{cm},
\]

\[
P_B=\frac{7.9^\circ}{20.8\ \mathrm{cm}}
=0.38\ ^\circ/\mathrm{cm}.
\]

Both solutions exhibit positive rotations of similar
magnitude, which is consistent with the expected
behaviour of sucrose solutions.

\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================

This experiment has allowed us to investigate the
Faraday effect and natural optical activity through
several experimental measurements.

First, the magnetic field generated by the
electromagnet was found to depend approximately
linearly on the electric current, following the
relationship

\[
B=(18.9061\ \mathrm{mT/A})I.
\]

The rotation of the polarization plane also increased
approximately linearly with the applied magnetic field.
Using the blue filter and a glass sample of length
$2.0$ cm, a Verdet constant of

\[
V_{\mathrm{blue}}=
0.0965\ ^\circ/(\mathrm{mT\,cm})
\]

was obtained.

The six spectral measurements generally showed a
larger rotation for shorter wavelengths, in agreement
with the expected approximate dependence
$V\propto\lambda^{-2}$. The global fit and the
comparison with literature values should nevertheless
be considered preliminary until the current $I_F$,
the exact filter wavelengths and the specific
reference data for TGP20 glass have been confirmed.

Finally, the Laurent polarimeter measurements yielded
optical rotation powers of $0.36$ and
$0.38\ ^\circ/\mathrm{cm}$ for samples A and B,
respectively. Both solutions showed positive and
similar optical rotations, consistent with the
expected behaviour of optically active sucrose
solutions.
