\section{Introduction}

The Pockels effect is a linear electro-optic phenomenon in which the
birefringence of an anisotropic material changes when an external
electric field is applied. This effect is related to the Kerr effect,
although in the latter case, the induced birefringence depends
quadratically, rather than linearly, on the electric field intensity.

For symmetry reasons, the Pockels effect can only occur in crystals
that lack a centre of inversion symmetry, such as lithium niobate
(LiNbO$_3$). This material is a negative uniaxial birefringent crystal
with refractive indices

\[
n_o = 2.29 \qquad \text{(ordinary)}
\]
\[
n_e = 2.20 \qquad \text{(extraordinary)}
\]

measured at the He-Ne laser wavelength
$\lambda_0 = 632.8\,\mathrm{nm}$.

Since the crystal is approximately $20\,\mathrm{mm}$ long, its total
birefringence can be expressed as

\[
\Delta n = n_e-n_o = -0.09.
\]

The corresponding number of wavelengths of phase difference across
the crystal is approximately

\[
\frac{L(n_o-n_e)}{\lambda_0}
=
\frac{20 \times 0.09}{632.8\times10^{-6}}
\approx 2840.
\]

In this experiment, the Pockels effect was investigated by observing
conoscopic interference patterns produced by the ordinary and
extraordinary waves propagating through the crystal.

By varying the voltage applied to the Pockels cell, the half-wave
voltage ($U_\pi$) was determined. This is the voltage variation
required to switch the transmitted light intensity from a maximum
to a minimum.

Furthermore, the system's response to small voltage variations was
experimentally investigated at different operating points. The
resulting light intensity variations were converted into an audible
signal, allowing the system's response to be studied acoustically.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Voltage Values at Maximum and Minimum Intensities
and Calculation of $U_\pi$}

Starting from the configuration
$\theta=45^\circ$ and $\alpha=0^\circ$, the voltage $U$ applied
to the Pockels cell was gradually increased. The maxima and minima
of the intensity at the centre of the conoscopic interference
pattern were then identified visually and recorded.

The voltage was measured using a voltmeter connected to a voltage
divider with a conversion factor of $8.3$. Consequently, the actual
voltage applied to the cell was obtained by multiplying the
voltmeter readings by this factor.

The resulting voltage values for each maximum and minimum are
presented in Table 1.

The half-wave voltage $U_\pi$ is defined as the voltage variation
required to move from a maximum to the consecutive minimum, or
vice versa.

Taking the average of the five consecutive voltage intervals
gives

\[
U_\pi =
\frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=1}^{5}|U_{i+1}-U_i|
=
\frac{1760.4-0.0}{5}
=
\mathbf{352.1\,V}.
\]

The individual voltage differences between consecutive extrema
are $457.3\,\mathrm{V}$, $351.1\,\mathrm{V}$,
$308.8\,\mathrm{V}$, $334.5\,\mathrm{V}$ and
$308.8\,\mathrm{V}$.

A certain degree of dispersion can be observed, particularly in
the first interval, whose voltage difference is considerably
larger than the remaining values. This discrepancy may be
attributed to the difficulty of visually identifying the exact
positions of the intensity extrema or to possible instrumental
limitations.

Nevertheless, all recorded measurements were included in the
calculation of the average half-wave voltage.

\subsection{Uncertainty and Error Analysis}

The voltmeter uncertainty was assumed to be
$\pm0.1\,\mathrm{V}$. Considering the voltage divider conversion
factor of $8.3$, the uncertainty in the applied voltage is

\[
\delta U = 8.3 \times 0.1 = 0.83\,\mathrm{V}.
\]

For each voltage interval, the propagated uncertainty is

\[
\delta D =
\sqrt{(0.83)^2+(0.83)^2}
\approx 1.17\,\mathrm{V}.
\]

Therefore, each individual voltage difference has an uncertainty
of approximately $\pm1.2\,\mathrm{V}$.

For the half-wave voltage calculated from the first and last
measurements, the propagated instrumental uncertainty is

\[
\delta U_\pi =
\frac{1}{5}
\sqrt{(0.83)^2+(0.83)^2}
\approx 0.23\,\mathrm{V}.
\]

However, the main source of uncertainty is the dispersion
between the five measured voltage intervals. Their sample
standard deviation is

\[
s =
\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{5}(D_i-\bar D)^2}{5-1}}
\approx 61.5\,\mathrm{V}.
\]

Consequently, the standard error of the mean is

\[
\delta U_\pi^{\mathrm{stat}}
=
\frac{s}{\sqrt{5}}
\approx 27.5\,\mathrm{V}.
\]

Since this statistical uncertainty is considerably larger than
the instrumental uncertainty, it dominates the final result.
Therefore, the half-wave voltage is reported as

\[
\boxed{U_\pi=(352\pm28)\,\mathrm{V}}.
\]

The relatively large uncertainty is mainly due to the first
interval, which differs significantly from the other
measurements.

\subsection{Explanation of the Observations in Section D2}

When the analyser was rotated from $0^\circ$ to $90^\circ$,
the intensity maxima and minima were observed to interchange.
This behaviour occurs because the transmitted intensities are
complementary:

\begin{align}
I(\theta=45^\circ,\alpha=0^\circ)
&=
\frac{E^2}{2}
\left\{1+\cos(\Delta\phi)\right\}
\\[1em]
I(\theta=45^\circ,\alpha=90^\circ)
&=
\frac{E^2}{2}
\left\{1-\cos(\Delta\phi)\right\}.
\end{align}

On the other hand, at an analyser angle of $45^\circ$, the
interference fringes almost completely disappeared. This occurs
because the analyser selects one of the principal modes of the
crystal, preventing interference between the two polarisation
components.

Finally, rotating the input polariser modified the contrast of
the observed patterns. When the incident polarisation was aligned
with one of the principal axes of the crystal, only one mode was
excited, and the intensity modulation disappeared.

\subsection{Discussion of the Acoustic Observations in Section D3}

When small voltage variations were applied, the resulting sound
was observed to be very weak at the intensity maxima and minima
(points b and c), whereas it became considerably louder in the
regions of maximum slope (point a).

This behaviour can be explained by the relationship

\begin{align}
    \delta I \simeq
    \frac{dI}{dU}\delta U.
\end{align}

At the extrema of the intensity curve, where
$dI/dU\simeq0$, the intensity modulation is minimal and the
sound almost disappears.

Conversely, in regions where the slope is maximum, even small
voltage variations produce significant changes in light intensity,
resulting in a stronger acoustic signal.

\section{Conclusions}

This experiment has allowed us to experimentally verify the
Pockels effect in a LiNbO$_3$ crystal by identifying the intensity
maxima and minima and determining a half-wave voltage of
$U_\pi=(352\pm28)\,\mathrm{V}$. The uncertainty is mainly
associated with the dispersion between consecutive voltage
intervals, while the instrumental contribution is negligible.

Despite the dispersion observed in some measurements,
particularly in the first interval, the results exhibit periodic
behaviour consistent with the theoretical predictions.

It was also confirmed that the transmitted light intensity
depends on the phase difference between the two polarisation
components, which is induced by the applied electric field.

Furthermore, the acoustic measurements qualitatively confirmed
that the intensity modulation is greatest in the regions with
the steepest slope of the $I(U)$ curve and smallest at its
extrema.

Overall, the experimental results are consistent with the
theoretical description of the Pockels effect and with the
expected behaviour of an electro-optic modulator.
